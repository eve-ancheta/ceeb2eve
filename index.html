From Ceeb to Eve

[This is a 2–3 minute read]



Hi, it's Eve.

When I came out as “Ceeb” in 2022, I wasn’t ready to choose a new name.

I didn’t know myself. It was just after COVID, and I was isolated: as a deeply anxious unschooler, I'd never taken a class, and I hadn't talked to other teens yet. I had zero idea that I was a girl (I came out as non-binary) because I’d never even met one. All I knew is that I wanted to be different, something other than a boy. So when I came out, I simply went with what I had at the time: “Ceeb,” my online name since 2017.

I spent nearly 4 years as “Ceeb.” In that time, I learned so, so much about myself: I’m an advocate, I’m a community builder, I’m a friend, I’m a partner, and apparently, I’m a Stanford superstar. Gender identity-wise, I learned that I’m a feminine girl.

I changed profoundly, yet “Ceeb” didn't reflect that. I think you'd agree. When people first hear “Ceeb,” they don’t think of a girl. Instead, they get confused. “Is it Ceb? Seed? Sea-Buh?” Speech-to-text thinks it’s “Sieb,” “Siri” and even “Verb" sometimes, and that makes it to texts. Once, at a homeschool gathering, I introduced myself and a mom announced, “Everybody! Steve is here!” (I didn't like it.)

Sometimes, I'm “Ceeb” and they think I'm another gender. “Ceeb shared his insights,” the opening line of a company newsletter recently read. Other times, I enter spaces and am assumed to be non-binary. I'm not. I don't want to be a “him,” and I don't want to be non-binary. Yet as “Ceeb,” in spite of how much I've changed, I constantly am. :(

I knew I needed to change my name. I thought carefully, and in 2024, I tested some candidates — you might’ve seen “CB” or heard “Cecelia.” None of them felt right, but I didn’t want to give up.

It took 19 more candidates and a rubric of 17 checkboxes. It took Namerology.com, Urban Dictionary, and article upon article covering name upon name. It took weeknights up at 4 AM, but guess what? One and a half years later, I finally settled on a name that I loved: “Eve.”

The feminine version of “Ceeb,” except it’s not a first-thought answer. A beautiful mathematical expression, e√e ≈ 4.48. Both people and digital transcription devices get it right. It fits that “Eve” means “new beginnings": as “Ceeb,” I entered high school, and as “Eve,” I'll fly into my new beginning of college (at Stanford, whose graduates founded Eve.com and Electronic Arts, the latter of which abbreviates to EA, my new set of initials; what interesting connections). It's a girl's name. And to me, it feels amazing!

I’m proud of the process that led me to Eve, yet there’s also something else. Looking back to where I began, I’m proud also of the growth that “Eve” reflects. Now, I’ve talked to other teens. Now, I’ve taken classes and lived as my fullest self out in the world. Now, I don’t feel awkward sharing my name to people, and in fact it’s the opposite, because this time, I really chose it myself. It feels more right than ever to step into this name. So now, as I’m “coming out” in 2026 — please, call me Eve! Unlike “Ceeb,” Eve is who I know I am. <3

Thank you for reading,

Eve Ancheta (she/her)
